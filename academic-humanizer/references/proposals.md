# Grant proposals (NSF, NIH, and similar)

The evidence and citation rules of SKILL.md apply to proposals unchanged. Agency requirements change between cycles, so the current solicitation and the agency's instructions take precedence over any structural statement below: for NSF, the PAPPG and its supplements, and for NIH, the funding opportunity and the application instructions. For other agencies, follow the agency's template and review criteria, and Sections 3 to 5 still apply.

## How a proposal differs from a paper

A proposal asks reviewers to fund work that has not been done, so its central claims concern a plan: what the team will attempt, why the attempt can succeed, and what the result would change. The claim and evidence rules of a paper become claim and feasibility rules (Section 5). Statements of a long-term goal and of expected impact, which Rule 1 removes from a paper, are expected in a proposal when the plan and the preliminary evidence support them. The audit script still lists words such as "transformative" and "pioneer"; keep each one only where its support appears in the same paragraph.

## 1. Structure

Reviewers usually form a view of a proposal from its opening pages and read the rest to check that view, so the opening pages deserve the most editing.

- **NSF.** The one-page Project Summary has three parts, Overview, Intellectual Merit, and Broader Impacts, each introduced by its heading on a line of its own and each understandable without the others. Within its first one or two pages, the Project Description should give the long-term goal, the goal of this proposal, the gap, the thrusts or aims, and the expected results, preferably with one overview figure. Broader Impacts should name concrete activities that are connected to the research.
- **NIH (R01 and other research project grants).** The Specific Aims page summarizes the proposal on one page and is the page most reviewers read closely. A common order runs from the problem, what is known, and the critical gap, through the long-term goal and the central hypothesis with its rationale, and the objective of this application, to two or three aims and what changes if they succeed. Each aim is a one-line goal, a phrase on the approach, and the expected outcome. The Research Strategy then has Significance, Innovation, and Approach sections. For due dates on or after January 25, 2025, most research project grants are reviewed under the simplified framework: Factor 1 (Importance of the Research, covering Significance and Innovation) and Factor 2 (Rigor and Feasibility, covering the Approach) are scored from 1 to 9, and Factor 3 (Expertise and Resources, covering Investigators and Environment) is rated only as appropriate or as having gaps.

## 2. What the opening pages must contain

By the end of page 1 (NIH Specific Aims) or pages 2 to 3 (NSF Project Description), the reader should know five things: why the problem matters, stated concretely, what is missing and what the gap costs, the central idea of the approach in one sentence, the aims or thrusts in parallel form, and the expected results. If any of these is missing or appears late, fix that before editing later sections.

## 3. Weak patterns to fix

- **Vague importance.** Watch for "this is an important and timely problem" and "X has many applications". State the specific consequence of the gap.

  Context supplied by the author: the proposal derives bounds that link measurement noise in wearable sensors to diagnostic error, and current diagnostic models that use such data are tuned by trial and error.

  Before: Understanding this problem is critically important.

  After: Diagnostic models that use wearable-sensor data are tuned by trial and error, because no available bound links measurement noise to diagnostic error. This proposal derives such bounds.

- **Method as aim.** An aim that names a technique instead of a question or an outcome.

  Before: Aim 2: Apply deep learning to camera-trap images.

  After: Aim 2: Determine for which species and image conditions an automated classifier identifies individual animals with a lower error rate than trained human coders.

- **Dependent aims.** If Aims 2 and 3 cannot proceed when Aim 1 fails, reviewers treat the plan as fragile. State the aims so that each produces a result on its own; where one aim depends on another, state the fallback.
- **Ambition without feasibility.** Each ambitious claim needs support placed next to it: preliminary data, a prior result or publication, a classical result the work builds on, or a collaborator's commitment. Write the support into the same sentence or the next one ("the preliminary result in Figure 2 shows ...").
- **Generic Broader Impacts or training plans.** Watch for "we will mentor students and disseminate the results through talks and papers". Replace with the specific programs, courses, software, or datasets that the plan contains, connected to the research, with counts where the plan gives them.
- **Hedged central hypothesis.** The hypothesis on the Specific Aims page is a falsifiable commitment ("We hypothesize that ..."), not "we will explore whether ... may ...". Hedging belongs in the Approach, where expected results and alternatives are interpreted.

## 4. Keep or add

- A long-term goal stated at the start, with this proposal as a defined step toward it.
- Run-in headings that make the text easy to scan: **Goal.**, **Motivation.**, **Innovation.**, *Aim N (one-line statement).*
- One concrete running example used across all aims.
- Each aim's central question stated as a question.
- The connection to named classical results that the work extends (a known inequality, policy class, or test), when the connection is real.
- The team's record as feasibility evidence, placed early: prior funded work, preliminary results, publications, collaborators, and partners. Use only the record the author supplied. Never invent funding, results, partners, or letters.

## 5. Claim and feasibility

For every aim and every promised outcome, check whether the ambition is matched by a stated means: preliminary data, a prior method, a classical foundation, a collaborator, or a staged plan with a fallback. If it is, keep the ambitious verb. If it is not, attach the missing support or narrow the claim to what the plan supports. When the support does not exist in the supplied material, insert `[[EVIDENCE: what is needed]]` and list it in the report; do not write around the gap.
